Blue Team Report

Team/Subgroup

Our team was Alan Phipps , Evelyn Smith , and
Douglas Kuschner .  We were protecting the host Mamet as
defined below.

Mission

Our mission, as we understood it, was to hide the flag in a decent place,
to try to make it difficult for host intruders to find and copy the flag
to their own machines, and (primarily) to detect any unauthorized accesses
of the flag.  We did not break up the group into precise responsibilities
on a per-person basis.

Preparation

Before the war, Alan developed and used a program to monitor whether or
not anyone accessed the real flag.  Upon flag access, the program echoed a
warning and wrote it in the /var/log/messages file as well.  It was quite
effective.

Before the war, Evelyn thought she could download the actual code for the
commands ls, grep, cat, etc. that have to do with viewing filenames and
file contents.  The plan was to render any file named "secret.txt"
effectively invisible.  She also wanted to seed Mamet with decoy flags.

Before the war, Doug wrote a program to detect spoofed IP packets
coming to Manet, keep track of MAC-IP associations from the traffic,
and give a warning upon detection of a spoofed packets (the next
version was to perform its own arp query on each incoming IP to verify
the MAC address).  This would have required installing JDK on Mamet,
had there been time and availability.  It looks as if it would have
been of definite help to us.

Execution

Alan developed and used a program to monitor whether or not anyone
accessed the real flag.  Upon flag access, the program echoed a warning
and wrote it in the /var/log/messages file as well.

The true flag's contents begin with "SECRET," unlike the contents of all
the decoy flags, which began with "secret"; all lines of the file began
with "SECRET" or "secret" in alternating lines, followed by alphanumerics.
False flags and the real flag, along with the new directories that in many
cases the flags were hidden in, were created by Evelyn.

Doug developed a program to detect and warn of IP spoofing.  By the
onset of the war, this program had not been installed or tested and
would have required the installation of JDK.  What with time and
availability problems, Doug didn't find the installation and testing
feasible.

Any obviously malicious processes were killed off.  "Obviously malicious,"
in this case, means that either we or another team managed to link some
malicious activity (e.g., a buffer overflow attack, or searching for
secret.txt) with a process and/or user.

Evaluation

Overall we did a pretty decent job.  It was about an hour and a half
before any real intrusion took place.  Our flag was accessed multiple
times, and (obviously) we succeeded in detecting these accesses.  We
therefore achieved our goal.

The change-the-commands-to-hide-secret.txt plan didn't work out.
Searching for the code files on Mamet was a horrible task and did not lead
to any results.  Searching on the internet gave lots of nice-looking
results.  Evelyn altered "ls" so that, she hoped, it would not show any
file named "secret.txt."  However, jubilation turned quickly to despair
once it became obvious that neither "ls" nor any other of the internet-
provided files was compatible with Mamet's setup.  Reluctantly Evelyn
concluded that this approach wouldn't be of any use.

On the whole, we didn't have too much excitement to begin with.  We got
scanned many times, and this scanning went on throughout much of the war.
We also were subjected to a huge number of buffer-overflow attacks,
especially ftp-buffer-overflow attacks from packard.cs.ucsb.edu.
Processing on Mamet became proportionally slow--and "slow" is an
understatement here.  We ran netstat and kept doing "who" and "finger" to
figure out who was playing with poor Mamet, and what exactly they were
trying to do.  Other than packard, snoop, garfield, and audit5 were three
machines that also seemed quite determined to get into Mamet.

At 2:19 pm the /etc/passwd file was modified, and our root process was
killed off.  We found that someone (probably from garfield) had changed
the root password, as well as the passwords of the two currently active
team user accounts.  It was a clever attack in that they also created
their own user account, bdr, and seemed to be using it to some effect,
since we saw multiple accesses of our real flag at this time, probably
from garfield and audit5, if we were to judge from the activity and
messages we got.  (At regular intervals, we copied /var/log/messages to
/.messages1, /.messages2, etc. so that any invaders to our machine could
not easily dispose of the evidence.)

Turnabout is fair play.  Having been kicked off our machine, we used a
(perl-based) buffer overflow attack in order to get back in.  It took
about 5 minutes.  Once in, we changed the root password and the two team
passwords, killed off all invading processes, and reclaimed our machine.
We justify this cyber- massacre because the naughty behavior was very
clear from the messages log and from the advice of other teams.  Also, it
had been our understanding that changing the root password had been
declared illegal.

We also changed the password of the newly created user "bdr", and the
messages file reflected this change because the creator attempted to log
in as "bdr" repeatedly.  We had a good laugh at this, but we didn't laugh
long:  soon "bd" was logged in to Mamet.  We weren't fooled--we knew it
was another interloper, but just as we were going to kill it off, we were
told by Giovanni that we shouldn't.  The system, he said, was supposed to
be left relatively insecure, as if we weren't there.  This viewpoint
contradicted what we'd previously thought, which was that we'd be allowed
to kill any processes that were obviously doing wrong.  We then backed off
and basically watched the rest of the war, at least until 2:50pm, at which
point we logged off to go to our 3pm classes.

Our flag was accessed roughly 7 or 8 times.  (I'd go back and count, but
my password is still mucked up, so I can't get in.)  This doesn't actually
tell us who managed to get a copy of the file.

We interacted with two or three of the nearby groups, giving them the
names of intruders we had detected and the attacks we believed they were
using.  They in turn notified us of intruder activity on Mamet.  It was
fairly effective in terms of communication.

Late in the game we tried to download an IDS from www.securityfocus.com to
play with, having realized that it was permitted, and that we'd have the
time to try.  We copied Adwids 0.8b1 over to Mamet using scp, but we
couldn't find it on Mamet.  We tried several times to no avail.  By this
time, it was quite out of the question to use ftp--too insecure.  Thus we
let this idea go.  We should have made sure what was permitted and what
was not, and we should have spent more time in setting up permitted
programs to monitor unauthorized activity.

Also, it would have been nice if we'd known who the teams were, known
what hosts we'd be on, met ahead of time, and had some concrete goals
to work with.  We had little time to coordinate and prepare.  As a
result we missed out on taking advantage of Doug's code, for example:
he didn't even get to mention it during the war, what with all the
other tactics we were trying, and the need for vigilance.

Furthermore, having the war and the class project and a homework
assignment due all on the same week really put a time crunch on us; it
would have been more fun if we could have at least not had a homework
assignment due this week.  (Other classes have things due this week,
too, of course, so that only worsened the situation.  That's why we
lost a team member: he took one look at the amount of stuff he had to
do this week for this class, and he knew he'd have to drop it.)

Some red team members started attacking before the war. This no doubt
made their task unfairly easy when wartime came.  In general, it
would've been nice if the rules of the war had been more clearly
stated.  There were multiple instances in which a clarification of the
rules would have saved everyone a lot of trouble.  Also, some form of
rule enforcement would've been nice.  Lots of people crashed hosts and
didn't even bother to notify the TAs; that sort of downtime severely
impacted the rest of the class.

Of course, it would have been nice if the red and blue teams could
have been put in separate rooms.  Installing the most basic security
precautions would also have been nice (shadow passwords, for example),
although I understand that the TAs were horribly pressed for time, and
the whole crashing thing made their lives as difficult as it made ours.
