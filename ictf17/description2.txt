You have just been hired to secure the critical infrastructure of
your country.

With the globally recognized threat of Sicilian hackers on the
offensive, you need to defend your country's critical services.

You will be give privileged access to a server that runs a number of
critical infrastructure services. Like all software, these services
probably have a number of undisclosed vulnerabilities.

These services are critical infrastructure, and therefore are used by
citizens of your country on a regular basis. As part of the normal
operation of a service, a piece of service-specific information,
called a "flag", is stored and retrieved from each service.

Due to the sneaky Sicilian hackers, amidst this normal, benign
traffic there are attacks! These attacks exploit vulnerabilities in
the services in order to retrieve flags that, in normal circumstances,
would not be accessible to the attacker.

Your goal is to identify which TCP connections contain an attack.
More precisely, our scoring system pushes to each team a flow
description (source and destination ports and IP addresses, timestamp,
and unique ID). The team can then call a scoring service and claim
that a certain connection (identified by its ID) contains an attack.

Correctly identifying a connection as malicious gives points to the
defending team.  The amount of points received depends on how timely
the detection is.  After a few hours, the detection is worthless.

Of course, false positives, i.e., identifying as malicious a
connection that is instead benign, will cause points to be subtracted
from the team's score (after all, who wants to have to do forensic on
a non-existent attack?).

Teams must identify vulnerabilities in their services and, if
possible, patch their services so that they are not vulnerable. Note
that if patching makes the service non-functional for the users (that
is, it does not work as expected when used in a benign way), the
service will be labeled as "non-functional". If the service is not
responding at all, the service will be labeled as "down". The amount
of services that are fully functional directly affect the points that
are assigned for correct detections. More precisely, the historical
values of availability creates a multiplier that is applied to the
overall score.

Little know to your commander-in-chief, the hat that you wear isn't
entirely white. It's more like an off-white or grey. A hacker's gotta
eat (and eat in style).

The way you earn your extra money (points) is to develop exploits and
sell them to the Zebra Dwarf Institution.

It is well known that the ZDI may use and run your exploits against
other nations. The ZDI requires that the exploit be written according
to a well-defined Python-based framework (soon to be published in
underground forums).

ZDI gives money for submitting a valid exploit, and they give
residuals for each successful flag captured from a service.

Note that, to prevent tarnishing your nation's reputation, you can't
launch attacks against other nations.  Instead, ZDI (the organizers'
infrastructure) will attack each team separately, using both known
exploits, and the exploits submitted by the various teams.

If the exploit written by nation X is successfully run against a
service of nation Y, points are given to nation X. If the attack is
detected by nation Y, of course, points are also assigned to nation
Y. Therefore, teams should devise stealthy attacks, and, at the same
time, effective patching and attack detection.

A scoreboard, shows, in real-time, the status of the services of each
team, and the amount of points accumulated. Note that the points can
decrease, because of false positives.

ZDI is very picky about the code they run. After all they are the ones
risking to get blown out of a building by a drone!  Therefore, ZDI
assumes that the code produced by you will be "well-behaved" (no DOS,
no attacks against the execution/scoring infrastructure). If such
behavior is detected in the attack code, the team will be blown up,
ahem, eliminated from the competition.
