Rules

The 2014-2015 Rules

The iCTF is a hacking competition open to educational institutions only.

Every team needs a faculty POC who is responsible for the ethical behavior of the team.

Most behaviors are acceptable. However, overtly-destructive behavior is frowned upon. Also, if you find a major issue with out infrastructure (it already happened...), please let us know instead of ruining the game experience for everybody. We'll send you kudos when the competition is over.

Denial-of-service attacks will be punished with the removal of the offending team from the competition.

The teams will receive an image with a number of vulnerable services. The task of the teams is to find the vulnerabilities and develop exploits for them.

The teams cannot directly attack each other. Instead, they submit their exploits to the organizers (see description of the process below), who, in turn, run the attack scripts against the various teams.

Scoring

You will have root on your VM, but you will not able to directly attack other teams. Instead, you will have to develop your exploits according to our framework (more information soon) and send them to us for testing. If they are functional, we will run your exploit on your behalf against the other teams.

Now, some notes about scoring. Read carefully.

The score for a team is a composition of four elements:

  • The Service Level Agreement (SLA), which takes into account which services are up and/or uncompromised during the competition.

  • The Timeliness score, which provides a bonus to teams that exploit a service earlier in the game.

  • The Diversity score, which provides a bonus to teams that exploit larger numbers of services.

  • The Leetness score, which provides points based on the number of teams that are exploiting a specific service.

More details about these components follow.

Service Level Agreement

During each tick in the game (say, 2 minutes), the scorebot determines the status of the services for each team.

A service can be down (that is, a TCP connection cannot be established) or non-functional (that is, something prevents the service from being used as intended. For example, a flag cannot be set or retrieved). In both cases, the team who owns the service gets 0 points for that service (but might be informed of the type of failure, to help diagnosing problems).

A service can be functional (which implies it is up). In this case, if the service has been compromised the team who owns the service gets 1 SLA point for that service. If the service was not compromised the team gets 2 SLA points. The overall SLA is the average of the SLA values across the whole competition.

For example, suppose that there are three services (S1, S2, S3) and three ticks (there will be a lot more, of course). These are the value for a specific team.

  • Tick 1: S1 is down = 0, S2 and S3 are up and uncompromised = 2 + 2 = 4. Total is 4
  • Tick 2: S1 is up but non-functional = 0, S2 is up and compromised = 1, and S3 is up and uncompromised = 2. Total is 3
  • Tick 3: S1 is up and uncompromised = 2, S2 and S3 are down = 0. Total is 2

At the end of the competition, the average SLA is (4 + 3 + 2) / 3 = 3.

At the end of the competition, the overall SLA is determined relative to the other teams. That is, the team who has the highest SLA (say 6) gets a score of 1. Everybody else gets a score that is proportional to the highest value. For example, the team in the example above has an SLA of 3 and therefore would get a score of 3/6 = 0.5.

This team’s final SLA is 0.5, which means that the overall score of this team (computed as a composition of timeliness, diversity, and leetness) will be cut in half.

Needless to say, it is very important that the team works hard to have their services up and functional (and possibly uncompromised).

Timeliness

The timeliness points are given to teams who submit early a working exploit to a service.

The first team gets full points (e.g., 10), the second team gets less points (e.g., 5), and so on. After a few iterations, no one else receives timeliness points for that service.

This point system gives a bonus to the fastest team to submit a working exploit (and penalizes those who copy it after having observed it on the wire).

At the end of the competition, the overall timeliness score is determined relative to the other teams. That is, the team who has the highest timeliness points (say 50) gets a score of 100. Everybody else gets scores proportional to the highest value (for example, a team that has 10 timeliness points would get a score of 10/50*100 = 20).

The timeliness score accounts for 20% of the overall score.

Diversity

The diversity points are given to teams in proportions to the number of different services they successfully exploit. If a team exploits a service, it will get 10 points. When the team submits a second service, the team will get 20 points (for a total of 30 points), for the third one, the team will get 30 points (for a total of 60 points).

At the end of the competition, the overall diversity score is determined relative to the other teams. That is, the team who has the highest diversity points (say 400) gets a score of 100. Everybody else gets scores proportional to the highest value.

The diversity score accounts for 40% of the overall score.

Leetness

Leetness points are given to the teams that are able to exploit the most difficult services.

At each tick there is a number of points (say 100) that are in play. These points are divided equally among the services that are being exploited in that tick. For example, if there are 5 services being actively exploited, each service will get 20 points. These 20 points are shared equally among the teams that are actively exploiting the service. So if a service is exploited by 2 teams, they will get 10 points each. If a service is exploited by 5 teams, they will each get 4 points each.

At the end of the competition, the overall leetness score is determined relative to the other teams. That is, the team who has the highest leetness points (say 2344) gets a score of 100. Everybody else gets scores proportional to this value.

The leetness score accounts for 40% of the overall score.

Summary

In summary, suppose that a team has an SLA of 0.5.

Suppose that the maximum amount of point for timeliness is 700 (that is, these are the timeliness points of the most timely team) and a team got 30 timeliness point. Their normalized timeliness score is then 30/700*100 = 4.28.

Suppose that the maximum amount of point for diversity scored by a team is 210 (that is, these are the diversity points of the most diverse team) and that the team exploited 5 services (150 diversity points). Their normalized diversity score is 150/210*100 = 71.4.

Finally, suppose that the team has gained 150 leetness points, and the best performing team gained 310 leetness points. Therefore their normalized leetness score would be 150/310*100 = 48.38

The overall score of the team would be (4.28 * 0.2) + (71.4 * 0.4) + (48.38 * 0.4) = 48.78. These is because of the relative weights of the scores.

This has to be me multiplies by the SLA. The team has an average SLA of 5.4, while the team with the best SLA has an average SLA of 7. Therefore the actual SLA score would be 5.4/7 = 0.77, and the final point would be: 48.78 * 0.77 = 37.63.

The team who has the highest overall final score wins. Note that there might a few parameter that we might change in order to balance the game, but this should be a pretty realistic description of our scoring procedure.

Submitting Exploits

In this iCTF, teams need to submit exploits to the organizers before they are actually run (by the organizers) in the game network. This email documents how the submission and vetting process work. It is similar to what we had last year. However, there are few, very subtle changes.

Submission Format

You can submit exploits in two different formats: a self-contained Python script, or a bundle.

Option 1: Python script submission

You need to provide a self-contained Python script that defines an "Exploit" class, according to the following interface:

class Exploit():
    def execute(self, ip, port, flag_id):
        [ ... ]
        self.flag = [ ... ]

    def result(self):
        return {'FLAG' : self.flag}

The execute() method gets as input the following arguments:

  • ip: a string that identifies the IP address of the target VM.

  • port: an integer that identifies the port of the target service.

  • flag_id: a string that tells the exploit which flag to steal.

The result() method does not take any arguments. It needs to return a dictionary. This dictionary needs to contain the "FLAG" key, and the value associated to it needs to a be the flag ("FLG....") stolen from the vulnerable service.

The execute() and the result() methods are mandatory. Everything else is up to you. As long as the result() method returns a dictionary as specified, you are good to go (assuming that the flag is the correct one, of course).

Your scripts will be run in a python virtual environment. Later on, we will tell you which libraries will be available.

Option 2: The bundle submission

You will be able to submit a bundle of Python files. The bundle must be a .tgz file. The bundle must contain a file called run.py, which will be used as entry point. The run.py python file is the one that needs to define the Exploit class. Of course, we will make sure your run.py file will be able to import additional files included in the bundle.

Flag ID: what the heck is that, and why do I need it?

Each of the services is designed to store multiple flag-looking strings. However, your exploit will need to steal only one of them! How do you know which one to steal? Well, the execute() method gets a flag_id as argument...

Here is the thing: a flag_id identifies the specific flag to capture. Thus, your exploit needs to steal the flag associated to the flag_id passed as argument!

The flag_id is service-dependent. For instance, if the flags are user passwords, the flag_id could be the specific username. As another example, if the flag is the status of the user, the flag_id could be the specific user_id. Note that you will not have to guess what the flag_id is for a service: The web interface will clearly state, for each service, a description of the flag_id.

You want to know more about this system? Check this paper out: http://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/pdfs/2014_3GSE_iCTF.pdf

Vetting Process

You will be able to submit your exploits through the web interface. Each of the exploit will be fired against a clean snapshot of the vulnerable VM. To test your exploit, we will first unpack it (in case you submitted a bundle), and we will then import the Exploit class, instantiate an Exploit object, and invoke its execute() method. Then, we will invoke the result() method, and get the flag out of the returned dictionary. If the flag is what we expect, you are good to go, and your exploit will be fired against all other teams. (Note: we also do a bunch of other checks, read below.)

In case your exploit has issues, informative feedback will also be provided through the web interface (e.g., a stack trace in case of an exception).

Testing / Examples

To get you started, you can get here an example of exploit for the echo service that is currently running on your VMs. We included both a python submission and a bundle submission. We also included a script (see setflag_for_echo.py) to setup the environment (i.e., set a flag to your echo service), and a script that runs the exploit against your target VM (see test_exploit.py). As the submissions to our infrastructure are rate-limited, we suggest you to test your exploits locally before submitting them to us. In this way, you will catch simple bugs early on.

Example:

Set a dummy flag for the echo service

$ ./setflag_for_echo.py <ip> TESTFLAG
Set a flag with FLAG_ID: ID-M3NG9
Try to get the flag with your exploit!

Then, lunch the exploit!

$ ./test_exploit.py exploit.py <ip> 9148 ID-M3NG9
Unpacked the exploit to /tmp/tmpTI1T1n/run.py
FLAG: TESTFLAG

or

$ ./test_exploit.py bundle.tgz <ip> 9148 ID-M3NG9
Unpacked the exploit to /tmp/tmpbcR4G0/run.py
FLAG: TESTFLAG

Rules

Your exploit must be written in Python. Please avoid to submit a blob then invoke os.system(). That's not going to fly.

If you submit a bundle, no pre-compiled bytecode is allowed. Don't include .pyc or .pyo files, or your submission will be rejected without mercy.

Naturally, the exploit needs to steal the correct flag. However, it must also not overwrite the current flag (we will run your exploit multiple times before accepting it!), and it must not damage the target service (yes, we check that as well as part of our vetting process).

The process is automated. However, we do monitor all submissions. Don't do anything nasty, or you will be banned. (Even worse, we will drive you crazy by rejecting your submissions and by providing obscure, uninformative, and misleading feedback. :))

As a final friendly reminder, all the attacks against the testing infrastructure and all attempts to fingerprint the system will NOT be tolerated. As we have all your submissions, it is easy for us to track who you are, and ban you from this (and future) iCTFs.
