Omega team, group B, detection and hiding of hosts.

Mission

Group B's offensive mission was to determine the IP addresses and operating system of the live hosts on the Alpha subnet and pass them on to group A.  Group B's defensive mission was to hide the IP addresses of our live hosts and distract the Alpha team from pursuing them.

Preparation

Swami researched tools to find Alpha's IPs and fingerprint them.  He looked into raw packet generator to try to find a way to bypass confusers and possibly evade snort.  These included Packet Builder, Packet Tools, Python LibNet, IP Packet Generator, EchoPing, Perl Advanced TCP Hijacker, TCPping, IP-Packet, HPing, Traffic, LibPal, SING, and NetCat.  He then created scripts to probe for live hosts and operating system version using Telnet, HTTP, and SNMP.  For the telnet scan he used TelnetFP to fingerprint the machines in addition to detecting their existance.  He also looked into the Remote OS fingerprint tool.  For the HTTP scan he echoed commands through NetCat.  For the SNMP scan he just sent mail.  By using parts of the protocols that would be unlikely to be emulated (such as HTTP POST, etc.), we would recognize confusers.  Swami also worked with Rajiv on a smart confuser that would forward packets to a real Linux machine running in a VM, UML, or on separate hardware, but it was lost when Omega's private (Will's network in Isla Vista) testbed was wiped out.

Gustavo researched existing confuser tools.  He looked at and dismissed Specter (commercial, Windows), BackOfficer Friendly (Windows), and Mantrap (commercial, Solaris).  The only reasonable looking open source Linux-based honeypots were honeyd and the Deception Tool Kit (DTK) which he evaluated much more extensively.  Honeyd was a big problem to compile, and once it finally did (on the day of the exercise), it refused to be successfully configured.  The DTK was fairly straightforward to compile and configure, but he ran into other problems with it.

Imran worked on running multiple copies of user mode Linux (UML) on our third machine to serve as honeypots.  He configured the filesystems and enabled the same services as were running on our real machines so as to make the UMLs nearly indistinguishable from them.  He also worked on a virtual LAN switch (smart confuser) that would forward traffic intended for the confuser IP addresses to the UMLs and filter any outbound traffic from them in case they were compromised.

Derek worked on a smart confuser that would pass traffic up to a copy of the Linux tcp-ip stack and also emulate various services, including Telnet, HTTP, FTP, and SMTP.  Integrating the Linux tcp-ip stack was too painful, and he didn't finish the service emulations in time for the exercise, so it ended up being a fairly simple confuser.  He also researched some low level confuser and obcuring tools, including IP Personality (changes TCP-IP stack characteristics) and the FPF loadable kernel module (it recognizes NMAP scans and can make NMAP detect any OS type we choose).  He looked into writing an IP Tables module as a forwarding confuser.  Derek also compiled the information for and wrote this report.

Rajiv researched tools for confusing and for fingerprinting.  The fingerprint tools included RING, Stealth Fingerprinter, passive fingerprinting, nmap, XProbe, QueSO, and IntraNode's paper on fingerprinting.  RING was the most promising, so he installed and configured it.  He looked into using the ECHO service (port 7) to detect confusers.  He also researched existing confusers such as LaBrea.  He worked with Swami on a smart confuser that would forward packets to a real Linux machine running in a VM, a UML, or on separate hardware.  He configured a simple confuser as a backup when the other confuser projects failed.

Execution

(NOTE: Evidence logs are provided at the end).

Defense

  • In addition to the three real machines, two IP addresses were allocated for the UMLs (128.111.43.99 and 128.111.43.100), and the remaining addresses were split between two different confusers.
  • Imran started three UMLs on our third machine and monitored them as they were probed and attacked.  Due to complaints about memory use, he killed some processes on the UMLs, but it broke their other services and it took half an hour to get them back up (by which time the attacks were concentrating on our real boxes).  He was able to determine that the Alpha team used FTP message scans to find the hosts, so once the UMLs FTP services went down, they were exposed as fakes.
  • Gustavo ran the DTK on the third machine, but due to a misconfiguration in the hosts.* files, it cut off all incoming traffic and he had to kill it.
  • Derek attempted to get his confuser running on the third box for half of the confuser IP addresses.  For some reason (despite being root) it was getting access denied errors when run.
  • Rajiv ran his confuser on the third box for the other half of the confuser IP addresses and monitored it as its addresses were probed.  He recorded over 23000 hits to those non-existant IP addresses!
Offense
  • Swami ran the Telnet probe at 4:02pm which immediately identified their live hosts (20, 17, and 11) and which operating system they were running.  There was no need to run his other scripts (HTTP 1.0, HTTP 1.1, SNMP).
  • After Swami identified the three live IP addresses, Gustavo ran an nmap OS fingerprint to verify the operating systems and return the open ports, although using the default options caused it to only list those below 6000.
  • We passed the IPs, OS versions, and open ports to the attack team so they could scan for specific vulnerabilities and attack them.
  • After Professor Vigna asked us why we hadn't run a port scan (Gustavo had done so), Swami ran another NMAP on hosts 17 and 11 at 4:22pm.
  • After Professor Vigna notified us that the buggy web server had been installed, Swami ran the HTTP GET script at 4:54pm against the open ports on host 17, but could only find the Apache web server (on 80 and 443).
  • We coordinated with the attack team so they could launch attacks from the UML VMs.

Evaluation

Our probing was extremely successful, as we identified the live IP addresses and operating system versions immediately (less than 30 seconds) and passed them to the attack group.

Our defense was also quite successful, as we logged many probes of both the confuser and the UMLs and we even logged some attacks on the UMLs.  We managed to give the Alpha team five non-confuser targets (two real, the third machine, and the UMLs) for at least the first 30 minutes (less time than we had hoped).  At that time, Alpha must have determined that the UMLs were not their intended target, as they stopped trying to break into them.  Despite the relatively short time that they were deceived, it showed that virtual (or user mode) machines could be an effective tool to confuse hackers and signal alarms.

The exercise would have been more challenging for our group if the IP address range had not been so restricted.  With only 10 addresses to check, it wasn't very difficult to find the real hosts.  This was made worse because the rules pertaining to the range of IP addresses were very vague till the end.  Since it had the same effect for the other team, it made all of our effort on confusers and virtual machines somewhat irrelevant.  Also, the inter-group communication was quite poor, and resulted in some confusion about who was doing what and when.  A dependancy graph between all the tasks assigned to all the groups, as well as expected inputs and outputs along each edge would have helped to organize the lab part of the exercise, and might have helped catch omissions like the vulnerability scans.

Miscellaneous

There was a misunderstanding regarding the ownership for the vulnerability scans. We thought that this would be done by Group A since they would have a better idea of the exploits they planned to execute. We successfully found the IP addresses, OS versions, performed the port scans, and passed the results to the attack group.  The vulnerability scans were omitted primarily due to confusion and lack of communication between the groups.

Derek collected all of the necessary information, logs, and opinions from the group members and compiled this report.
